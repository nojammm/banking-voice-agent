# Government rules: payments, unauthorised transactions, credit and unclaimed money

The ePayments Code is a code of practice administered by ASIC, the Australian Securities and Investments Commission. ASIC describes it as presently a voluntary code, and most banks, credit unions and building societies subscribe to it. The ePayments Code covers electronic payments such as ATM, EFTPOS and credit card transactions, online and mobile banking, and BPAY. The current version has applied since 2 June 2022. It sets rules on who pays for unauthorised transactions and how mistaken internet payments are handled.

## Who pays for unauthorised transactions under the ePayments Code

Under the ePayments Code (effective 2 June 2022), an account holder is not liable for an unauthorised transaction where it is clear the user did not contribute to the loss, or where the loss was caused by fraud or negligence of bank staff or merchants, forged, faulty, expired or cancelled cards or passcodes, transactions before the card or passcode was received, duplicate debits, or transactions after loss or theft was reported. The bank must prove on the balance of probability that the user contributed through fraud or a passcode security breach, and a breach alone is not enough. Where a passcode was required and no other rule applies, liability is limited to the least of $150 (or lower), the available balance, or the actual loss when reported.

Under the ePayments Code (effective 2 June 2022), a user who unreasonably delays reporting a lost or stolen card or device, or a compromised passcode, can be liable for losses that occur during the delay, subject to transaction limits. An account holder is not liable for losses from unauthorised transactions that happen after the user has reported the loss, theft or misuse of the card or device, or the breach of passcode security.

## Mistaken payment to the wrong account: how to get money back

-->
Under the ePayments Code (effective 2 June 2022), a mistaken internet payment should be reported to your own bank, and the process depends on when it is reported. Within 10 business days, if the funds are available, the receiving bank must return them within 5 business days if practicable and no later than 10 business days, without needing the recipient's consent. Between 10 business days and 7 months, the receiving bank investigates within 10 business days and, if satisfied, freezes the funds for 10 further business days, returning them unless the recipient proves they are entitled to them. After 7 months, funds are returned only if the unintended recipient consents.

Under the Banking Code of Practice (effective 28 February 2025), a customer can complain about a bank's banking services or its compliance with the Code. If a bank that subscribes to the Code cannot resolve a complaint within 30 days, or 21 days if it involves a hardship notice, default notice or request to postpone enforcement under the National Credit Code, the bank will tell the customer the reasons for the delay and the date by which they can reasonably expect to hear the outcome, give monthly progress updates, and tell them about the Australian Financial Complaints Authority (AFCA).

Moneysmart says you have a right to get a copy of your credit report for free every 3 months, or more often if you have recently been rejected for credit. Moneysmart names Experian and Equifax as the two main credit reporting bodies. Defaults stay on a credit report for five years, or seven years for a clearout, and repayment history covers the last two years. Getting errors on a credit report fixed is a free service.

## Responsible lending: a lender can't give you an unsuitable loan
<!--

verified: 2026-10-28

Under Australia's responsible lending obligations, administered by ASIC, credit licensees such as banks must not enter into a credit contract that is unsuitable for the consumer. They must make reasonable inquiries about the consumer's financial situation and their requirements and objectives, take reasonable steps to verify the consumer's financial situation, and assess that the contract is "not unsuitable". The consumer can request a written copy of the assessment.

ASIC, the Australian Securities and Investments Commission, says money from old bank accounts that a bank cannot return to the owner after seven years of inactivity is held by ASIC if it is more than $500, while claims related to old bank accounts under $500 should be made with the bank. People can search for unclaimed money using ASIC's free Moneysmart search tool. ASIC says the search and claims process is completely free, and there is no deadline to make a claim.

## Scams Prevention Framework: new legal duties for banks

ASIC says the Scams Prevention Framework imposes enforceable obligations on banks and other regulated businesses to prevent, detect, disrupt, report and respond to scams. Most Scams Prevention Framework obligations commence on 31 March 2027. The Australian Financial Complaints Authority (AFCA) is the external dispute resolution scheme for Scams Prevention Framework complaints, and covered businesses had to be AFCA members by 1 September 2026. AFCA is a free and independent dispute resolution service.

Moneysmart says anyone who has been scammed should contact their bank or card provider immediately to report the scam, report the scam to Scamwatch to help protect others, change passwords, and contact IDCARE on 1800 595 160 for a free response plan. Moneysmart warns to be wary of anyone who claims they can recover your losses for a percentage of the recovered losses, as this is a common follow-up scam. If unhappy with the bank's response, the customer can make a formal complaint to the bank and then to the Australian Financial Complaints Authority (AFCA).
