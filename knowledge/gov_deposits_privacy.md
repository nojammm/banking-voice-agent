# Government rules: deposit protection, privacy, credit reporting, scams law and open banking

## Financial Claims Scheme: government protection for bank deposits up to $250,000

The Financial Claims Scheme (FCS) is an Australian Government scheme that protects deposits in authorised deposit-taking institutions (ADIs), such as banks, building societies and credit unions, up to $250,000 per account holder per ADI. APRA, the Australian Prudential Regulation Authority, says the FCS covers ADIs incorporated in Australia and authorised by APRA. The FCS can be activated by the Australian Government in the unlikely event that an ADI fails, and APRA administers it once activated.

APRA says the Financial Claims Scheme limit of $250,000 applies to all of a person's deposits held with one authorised deposit-taking institution (ADI), even when that ADI operates several brands under one banking licence. APRA's examples: St George is part of Westpac, and deposit accounts offered by RAMS are actually Westpac accounts. A person's deposits across these brands are added together and protected up to one $250,000 limit.

APRA says a person can have up to $250,000 in deposit accounts with a number of different authorised deposit-taking institutions (ADIs), and each is protected separately under the Financial Claims Scheme. For joint accounts, the amount of Financial Claims Scheme protection is worked out by splitting the deposit equally between the account holders; each account holder's share is then added to any other eligible deposits they hold under their own name at the same ADI.

APRA says the Financial Claims Scheme covers savings accounts, call accounts, term deposits, cheque and transaction accounts, cash management accounts, trustee accounts, and mortgage offset accounts (100 per cent or partial offset) that are separate deposit accounts. It does not cover accounts with funds not in Australian dollars, accounts kept at overseas branches of Australian banks, credit balances on credit cards or other loans, or prepaid card facilities and similar products.

If the Financial Claims Scheme is activated, APRA says it will endeavour to make payments to the majority of account holders within seven calendar days. APRA and the failed institution will tell depositors any steps they need to take to access their payment. Payments are only made in Australian dollars and, in most cases, by cheque or electronically to an alternate account nominated by the account holder. An account holder can make a claim in the institution's liquidation to recover an amount over the $250,000 FCS limit; such claims rank third in priority.

The Office of the Australian Information Commissioner (OAIC) says Australian privacy law gives you a general right to access your personal information, except where the law allows an organisation to refuse your request. The OAIC considers 30 days a reasonable period to respond to an access request. An organisation may charge for giving access, but the charge can't be excessive and can't be used to discourage you from requesting access. If it refuses access, it must tell the person in writing its reasons for refusing and how they can make a complaint.

The Office of the Australian Information Commissioner (OAIC) says Australian privacy law gives people the right to have personal information corrected if it is inaccurate, out of date, incomplete, irrelevant or misleading, and there is no charge for correction. The OAIC considers 30 days a reasonable time to respond. If an organisation refuses to correct the information, the person can ask it to attach a statement saying the information is wrong, and the organisation must take reasonable steps to do so.

The Office of the Australian Information Commissioner (OAIC) says a person must first complain to the organisation they think mishandled their personal information. If the organisation does not respond within 30 days, or the person is unhappy with the response, they can complain to the OAIC; the Privacy Act 1988 requires the complaint to the OAIC to be in writing. For a privacy complaint about a bank, the OAIC says you should contact the Australian Financial Complaints Authority (AFCA), which has the power to deal with your privacy complaint.

Under the Notifiable Data Breaches scheme, explained by the Office of the Australian Information Commissioner (OAIC), an organisation covered by the Privacy Act has to tell affected people, and the OAIC, if a data breach is likely to cause them serious harm. Generally, an organisation or agency has 30 days to assess whether a data breach is likely to result in serious harm. A notification includes the organisation's contact details, the kinds of information involved, a description of the breach, and recommended steps people can take. Examples of serious harm include identity theft, which can affect finances and credit reports, and financial loss through fraud.

The Office of the Australian Information Commissioner (OAIC) says a credit reporting body must give a person access to their consumer credit report for free once every 3 months. A free copy is also available if the person has been refused credit within the past 90 days, or if their credit information has been corrected. The OAIC lists Equifax (138 332) and Experian (1300 783 684) as credit reporting bodies, and notes that credit reporting bodies may hold different information about a person.

The Office of the Australian Information Commissioner (OAIC) says a person who finds an error on their credit report should contact the relevant credit provider, such as the bank, in the first instance, or can ask a credit reporting body to correct it; correction is free. An agreed correction must be made within 30 days, or a longer period the person agrees to. If the correction request is refused, the person must be told in writing and told they can access an external dispute resolution scheme or make a complaint.

The Office of the Australian Information Commissioner (OAIC) says a person who believes they are, or are likely to be, a victim of fraud, including identity theft, can ask a credit reporting body for a ban period. The ban period lasts 21 days after the request and is free. There is no limit on the number of times a ban period can be extended. During a ban, the credit reporting body generally cannot use or disclose the person's credit report without their written consent.

The Australian Government has formally authorised the Australian Financial Complaints Authority (AFCA) to handle external disputes under the Scams Prevention Framework across the banking, telecommunications and digital platform sectors. The Office of the Australian Information Commissioner (OAIC) says the Consumer Data Right allows you to give an accredited business access to your consumer data. The Consumer Data Right is an opt-in system and is available in the banking sector. A person can withdraw their consent at any time, and can ask for their data to be deleted if it is no longer needed.
